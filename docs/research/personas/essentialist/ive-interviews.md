# Jonathan Ive: interview and keynote material — Objectified (2009), The New Yorker (2015), "Designed by Apple in California" (2016)

Per the brief, this file prioritizes Ive's own words on process, hierarchy of
importance, and care for unseen detail over biography. The New Yorker profile itself
could not be directly fetched (paywalled); what follows for that source is quotes
confirmed via a secondary summary, flagged as such below.

## 1. Objectified (Gary Hustwit, 2009) — Ive's on-camera lines

Source note: Hustwit also directed the 2018 Dieter Rams documentary covered
separately in `hustwit-rams-2018.md`; Objectified is an earlier, different film in
the same director's design-documentary work, and is not to be confused with it.

> "A big definition of who you are as a designer is the way you look at the world."

> "So many of the products that we're surrounded by want you to be very aware of
> just how clever the solution was."

> "A lot of what we are doing is getting design out of the way."

Operational reading: the second and third quotes together describe a specific
failure mode Ive names and rejects — design that visibly signals its own cleverness
to the user — and its intended replacement: design whose presence the user doesn't
have to notice at all. This is a hierarchy-of-attention claim, not just a taste
preference: cleverness competing for the user's attention is treated as a design
defect.

## 2. The New Yorker, "The Shape of Things to Come" (Ian Parker, 23 February 2015)

Source-quality note: the original article (a ~17,000-word profile) sits behind The
New Yorker's paywall and was not reachable via WebFetch this session. The quotes
below were confirmed via idownloadblog.com's summary of the piece, which reproduces
them as direct quotations; the surrounding paragraph-level context (exactly what
question prompted each line) was not independently re-verified against the original
article.

On Apple Watch design constraints — a function-drives-form argument:

> "How do we create a huge range of products and still have a clear and singular
> opinion?"

> "When a huge part of the function is lists of names or appointments, a circle
> doesn't make any sense."

Operational reading: the second quote is a direct, function-first argument against a
circular watch face (the format several competitors used) — the rejection is framed
as a legibility/function problem (lists of text don't fit a circle well), not a
stylistic one.

On the Digital Crown's positioning, rejecting a literal/skeuomorphic reference:

> "It's just literal. And you could say, 'Why is that an issue?' Well, if it's
> literally referencing what's happened in the past, the information about what it
> does is then wrong."

Operational reading: this connects directly to the iOS 7 flattening rationale
documented in `ive-apple-cases.md` — Ive rejects a design element for being a literal
reference to a prior mechanical convention when that reference no longer describes
what the part actually does.

On working through icon geometry at extreme, generally-invisible levels of detail:

> "They drove me crazy. All I could see were these unresolved tangency breaks."

Operational reading: "tangency breaks" refers to the mathematical smoothness of a
curve where two arcs meet — a defect invisible to virtually every user, but one Ive
describes as impossible for him to ignore. This is the clearest single-line evidence
in this file for "care for unseen detail" as a lived practice rather than a slogan.

On the difficulty of turning intuition into articulated, shareable reasoning (in the
context of his working relationship with Steve Jobs):

> "My intuition's good, but my ability to articulate what I feel was not very
> good — and remains not very good, frustratingly."

## 3. "Designed by Apple in California" — Ive's introduction (Apple, 2016)

This limited-edition book anthologized twenty years of Apple product photography;
Ive wrote its introduction. Quotes below are confirmed via danielscrivner.com's
direct excerpting and a separate WebSearch excerpt (for the printing/color passage,
whose original context — a description of the book's own production — is noted
inline).

On design and manufacturing as one activity, not two:

> "Designing and making are inseparable."

> "Fundamental ideas and shapes are derived directly from our knowledge of materials
> and manufacturing processes."

On simplicity as communication, restated from the same "not the absence of
complexity" formulation Ive used in Rams' foreword (see `ive-rams-foreword.md`):

> "Simplicity is not the absence of complexity. Just removing clutter would result
> in an uncomplicated but meaningless product."

On care as something users perceive rather than something merely claimed:

> "Human beings sense care in the same way we sense carelessness."

On the book's own production running into Apple's color/material restraint as a
literal printing problem (paraphrase, with one embedded direct clause): describing
why the book needed custom paper stock and custom inks, Ive is quoted explaining that
"many of these products are white, so the off-the-shelf printing processes really
didn't do an adequate job in describing the color and surface of those products" —
i.e., achromatic material fidelity was treated as worth solving with a bespoke
manufacturing process for the book itself, the same standard applied to the products
it depicts.
