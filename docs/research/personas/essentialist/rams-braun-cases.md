# Concrete Braun/Vitsœ product decisions: what was removed, standardized, and colored

Per the brief, this file prioritizes operational, product-level evidence over
biography: specific removals, specific standardizations, and specific
typeface/color decisions, each attributed to a source.

## Case 1 — Braun SK4 "Phonosuper" record player (1956, with Hans Gugelot)

### What was removed (paraphrase, cross-checked across multiple secondary sources)

Paraphrase: Domestic radio-phonograph combinations of the era ("radiogramophones")
were conventionally housed in fully wooden cabinets, often ornamented, styled to
read as furniture. Rams and Gugelot's original plan replaced this with a body made
of white lacquered sheet metal — cheaper than a wooden cabinet — with only the two
side panels left in wood (maple). The all-wood cabinet convention is the thing
removed; what replaced it was a mostly-metal chassis with wood retained only at the
sides.

### What was standardized / the notable material substitution

Paraphrase: The metal lid originally planned for the top rattled because the
built-in loudspeaker's vibrations set it in motion. Rams' resolution was to
substitute a transparent acrylic (perspex) cover instead of solving the rattle
within the metal design — i.e., the fix was a material substitution up front, not a
later mitigation. This transparent lid is what earned the product its nickname,
quoted verbatim from a design-history source describing it: "Snow White's Coffin."

Direct quote (design-history secondary source, cross-checked): the SK4 is described
as

> "a real innovation in a market dominated by huge radiogramophones."

### Color/material decision

Paraphrase: the final production object combined white metal housing, natural
maple wood side panels, and the clear acrylic lid — a deliberate reduction to three
materials/finishes (white lacquer, wood, clear plastic) rather than the varied wood
veneers and finishes typical of contemporary radiogram cabinets.

Caveat: sources located in this session describe the SK4's design history and
material substitution consistently across at least two independent secondary
sources (a design-history blog post and a manufacturer-affiliated history page,
braunaudio.de), but no primary Rams-authored statement specifically about the SK4's
design reasoning was reachable this session — this case entry is paraphrase from
secondary design history, not a Rams quote, and should be weighted accordingly.

## Case 2 — Braun color system across the audio product line (source: rams-foundation.org, "Colors" — official Rams Foundation article, retrieved via WebFetch)

This is the primary evidence for the "single color accent on an otherwise achromatic
device" pattern named in the task brief.

Direct quote — the functional color chart:

> "FM buttons should be 'blood orange', phono buttons 'honey yellow', with all power
> buttons 'leaf green'."

Direct quote — the reasoning for that specific chart entry (regie 500):

> "This function, which supports operation, is easy to understand because it
> creates a link between the orange FM button of the regie 500 and the scale digits
> in the same colour."

Direct quote — Rams' own stated rationale for restraining color use at all:

> "Colour can dominate a room. And design shouldn't dominate people, it should help
> people."

Rams is also quoted as being "very cautious" about color, accepting it only when
colors "have to suit the product" (both short phrases quoted verbatim as retrieved).

Operational reading for persona distillation: color in this system is never
decorative — every color used is tied to a single semantic (which function this
button performs), assigned from a small fixed vocabulary (blood orange / honey
yellow / leaf green), applied consistently across the product line so the same
color always means the same function across different devices ("push-button
switches" only, per the source). This is the documented mechanism behind "the
single green power switch as the only color accent on an otherwise achromatic
device" pattern referenced in the task brief — green specifically maps to power
across the audio line per this chart.

## Case 3 — Braun ET66 calculator (1987, with Dietrich Lubs)

### Color decision (source: V&A Collections curatorial record, retrieved via
WebFetch)

Paraphrase + quote: the calculator body is black plastic; number buttons are black,
mathematical-symbol buttons are brown, and the equals button — described as "the
most frequently used function" — is the single yellow accent. Direct quote from the
curatorial text:

> "The interface is cleverly colour-coded for ease of use, with the eye drawn to the
> yellow 'equals' button, the most frequently used function."

Operational reading: this is the same pattern as Case 2 (single accent color
reserved for the operationally most-important control) applied to a different
device 30 years later — evidence the color-accent rule was a durable, repeated
practice rather than a one-off choice on one product.

Caveat: the V&A record, per its own text, "does not specify typeface or lettering
details" for the ET66 — no typeface claim is made for this specific product in this
file; see the general Braun typeface note below instead.

## Braun typeface standardization (general, across the product line and printed
matter)

Source note: this is aggregated from WebSearch excerpts of multiple secondary
design-history sources (a graphic-design trade press piece on Otl Aicher/Braun, and
general design-history summary); no single primary Rams or Aicher statement was
directly fetched in this session, so treat as paraphrase, cross-checked across
multiple independent secondary sources rather than a direct quote.

Paraphrase: Otl Aicher, brought in to overhaul Braun's corporate identity in 1955
(Rams was part of that same design team), initiated Braun's use of the sans-serif
typeface Akzidenz-Grotesk as the standard for all Braun communications — catalogues,
prospectuses, advertisements — and this same typeface carried through onto product
lettering and dials, mostly set in all-lowercase on work designed solely by Rams.
Aicher and the Ulm School of Design (HfG Ulm) collaborated with Braun through the
late 1950s–60s; Aicher is credited with designing the dials, displays, and lettering
across Braun devices. (Note: Aicher's own later, separate signature typeface,
Univers by Adrian Frutiger — used for the 1972 Munich Olympics identity — is a
different project and not the Braun in-house standard; flagging this so the two are
not conflated when distilling a "typeface law.")

## Case 4 — Vitsœ 606 Universal Shelving System (1960, source: vitsoe.com/us/606,
retrieved via WebFetch)

### What was standardized

Direct quote — the tool-less mounting mechanism:

> "Shelves, cabinets and tables are simply hung with a pin from the E-Tracks –
> without the need for tools."

Direct quote — the combinatorial system built from exactly two standardized bay
widths:

> "There are 27 possible bay combinations for a 16ft-wide wall."

Paraphrase: the two bay widths are 65cm (narrow) and 90cm (wide); every
configuration option in the entire system is built by combining just these two
standardized units, rather than offering custom widths.

### What was removed

Paraphrase: no visible fixings — the E-track/pin mounting is designed so shelving
attaches without exposed screws or brackets, and no tools are required for the user
to reconfigure the system themselves (per the direct quote above).

### Color standardization — the explicit "only these colors, permanently" rule

Direct quote:

> "We do not pander to fashion: our products and colors will never become
> obsolete."

Paraphrase: only six color-combinations and four individual colors are offered, on
a permanent basis: off-white, black, silver, and beech. This is presented as a
closed, standing set — not a seasonal palette that gets refreshed.

### Longevity as designed-in system behavior, not marketing language

Direct quote — the stated usage principle:

> "Start small. Add to it. Take it with you. Reconfigure it. Hand it on."

Direct quote — the sales-side version of the same principle:

> "buy less, initially, and only add more later if you need to."
