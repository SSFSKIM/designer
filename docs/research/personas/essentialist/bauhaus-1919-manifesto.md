# Bauhaus Manifesto and Program — Walter Gropius, Weimar, April 1919

> Lineage identity only — expressionist-craft phase ("the cathedral of the future"); do NOT cite under functionalist laws.

This file exists so a later distillation task can place the Bauhaus's 1926 functionalist
turn (see `bauhaus-functionalist-texts.md`) in its actual historical sequence — starting
from a founding document that is mystical and craft-guild in temperament, not yet
functionalist. Nothing here should be read as evidence for standardization,
function-derived form, or economy-of-means laws; that grounding comes from the 1923
and 1926 texts instead. This document's job is only to establish what the Bauhaus was
*before* it became the thing the persona actually draws on.

## The founding paragraph (direct quote, in full — this is the passage the "cathedral
of the future" framing refers to)

> "The ultimate aim of all visual arts is the complete building! To embellish buildings
> was once the noblest function of the fine arts; they were the indispensable
> components of great architecture. Today the arts exist in isolation, from which they
> can be rescued only through the conscious, cooperative effort of all craftsmen.
> Architects, painters, and sculptors must recognize anew and learn to grasp the
> composite character of a building both as an entity and in its separate parts."

> "Architects, sculptors, painters, we all must return to the crafts! For art is not a
> 'profession.' There is no essential difference between the artist and the craftsman.
> The artist is an exalted craftsman."

> "Let us then create a new guild of craftsmen without the class distinctions that
> raise an arrogant barrier between craftsman and artist! Together let us desire,
> conceive, and create the new structure of the future, which will embrace
> architecture and sculpture and painting in one unity and which will one day rise
> toward heaven from the hands of a million workers like the crystal symbol of a new
> faith."

Paraphrase: that closing image — a building rising "like the crystal symbol of a new
faith," conceived by "a million workers" as a single guild without artist/craftsman
distinction — is the textual referent of Feininger's cover woodcut, a crystalline
Gothic cathedral radiating three stars. The register here is explicitly religious and
collective ("guild," "faith," "crystal symbol"), not industrial or economic. There is no
mention of machines, standardization, or mass production anywhere in this founding
paragraph — a deliberate contrast with the 1923 and 1926 texts, where those become the
central vocabulary.

## What the rest of the program document covers (paraphrase — not quoted, since it is
curriculum administration rather than design philosophy)

Paraphrase: the balance of the four-page document — roughly three-quarters of its
total length — lays out the Bauhaus's organizational structure: the 1919 merger of the
Weimar arts academy and the arts-and-crafts school, the three-tier apprentice/
journeyman/junior-master training system modeled explicitly on medieval craft guilds,
a full list of workshop trades taught (stone, wood, metal, ceramics, glass, textiles),
a parallel curriculum in drawing/painting and in art-historical and scientific theory,
and closing administrative detail (tuition of 180 marks/year, admission open
"without regard to age or sex"). None of this is quoted here because none of it bears
on design *laws* — it is governance and pedagogy, useful only as background for why a
craft-guild school could later pivot toward Gropius's 1923 "Art and Technology: A New
Unity" reorientation covered in `bauhaus-functionalist-texts.md`.

## Why this file must not be cited under functionalist laws

Paraphrase, stating the point the warning line above compresses: in 1919 Gropius's
model for unifying design was the medieval cathedral-building guild — craftsmen and
artists merged under one spiritual, collective calling, taught by hand in workshops,
with no functionalist or industrial vocabulary present at all. The functionalist
turn — standardization, "an object is defined by its nature," design as the outcome of
studying an object's function rather than an artist's expressive impulse — is a
distinct, later development, documented instead in Gropius's 1923 Weimar exhibition
essay and the 1926 "Principles of Bauhaus Production" text. Citing this 1919 document
as if it already contained those functionalist commitments would misdate the Bauhaus's
actual intellectual history by several years; that is the specific error this file's
warning line is meant to prevent.

## Source-quality note

The PDF at the pinned URL is an unattributed course handout (hosted on a university
student's personal weebly site) rather than an original scholarly edition, but its
translation matches — word for word, including distinctive phrasing like "crystal
symbol of a new faith" — the excerpt independently quoted at bauhausmanifesto.com and
the manifesto text hosted on academia.edu. This is the same English translation
lineage that traces back to Hans M. Wingler's *The Bauhaus* (MIT Press, 1969) and
Ulrich Conrads' *Programs and Manifestoes on 20th-Century Architecture* (MIT Press,
1970) — the standard anthologized English version of this text — so despite the
informal hosting, the wording itself is the widely-cited standard translation, not an
idiosyncratic paraphrase.
